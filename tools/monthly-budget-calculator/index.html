Budget questions, answered

What is the 50/30/20 rule?

Split your take-home pay three ways: about 50% on needs, about 30% on wants, and about 20% on savings and paying down debt. Needs are the things you cannot skip, like housing, utilities, groceries, insurance and getting to work. Wants are everything you choose, like dining out, subscriptions and travel. The last 20% builds an emergency fund, goes into retirement, or kills debt faster.

Do I use gross pay or take-home pay?

Take-home pay, the amount that actually lands in your account after tax and payroll deductions. If your employer already moves money into a retirement plan before you see it, that saving is happening outside this calculation, so count it as a bonus rather than part of your 20%.

Is 50/30/20 realistic with high rent?

Often not. In expensive areas needs alone can run 60% or more, which is why the targets here are editable. If needs will not fit under 50%, the useful question is which single number can move: a cheaper housing cost, a roommate, a shorter commute, or more income. Lowering the wants bucket for a month works; lowering it forever usually does not.

Where do minimum debt payments go?

Strict 50/30/20 treats minimum payments as needs, because missing them has real consequences, and counts only the extra you pay above the minimum in the 20%. This calculator starts with debt in the savings and debt bucket because that is how most people think about it, and the dropdown next to each category lets you move it.

What if I have nothing left over?

That is the normal starting point, and it is still useful information: it tells you the plan has no slack, so any surprise becomes debt. Look at the biggest 5 categories first, since a 10% cut to your largest line beats a 50% cut to your smallest. Then give the leftover a job on purpose, even if it is only $25 a month, so it does not quietly get spent.
